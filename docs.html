Conservancy Council

A role-play simulation of integrated water resources management (IWRM) for a coastal water conservancy in Guyana. This manual covers the rules, the theory behind the game, and every equation the model uses, for both water quantity and water quality.

Version 2.2 · November 2026Faculty of Earth and Environmental Sciences, University of Guyana6 teams + 2 optional upstream teams · 8 seasons · about 2 hours

01Overview

Six stakeholder teams share one water body: a coastal conservancy that stores rainfall runoff from its catchment behind an earthen dam. The conservancy supplies Georgetown's drinking water and irrigation for rice and sugar. In wet seasons it threatens the dam, and the operator must release water through relief sluices into the Mahaica River, where downstream villages farm the floodplain.

Each round is one season. Teams read a rainfall outlook, negotiate, and set their decisions. The computer model then calculates the season's water balance: how much water came in, how much each user received, how much was released, whether the dam was overtopped, and who paid for it. Over eight seasons (two years) the teams live with the consequences of their own choices and of each other's.

Water quality runs alongside quantity. Coastal rice and sugar farms choose how much fertilizer to use; their runoff drains through canals and kokers to the estuary, where nitrogen threatens marine life. The conservancy's own water quality depends on what happens upstream in its catchment: farms add nitrogen, and gold mining and land clearing add sediment. The upstream basin lies outside the council's control. The facilitator can run it as a fixed scenario nobody at the table can influence, then hand it to two upstream teams to show what changes when both basins talk.

Inspired by, not a model of, a real system. The setting is based on the kind of choices faced at coastal conservancies such as the East Demerara Water Conservancy. All default numbers are illustrative and chosen for teaching. Section 13 explains how to replace them with real data.

02Learning objectives

After playing and debriefing, participants should be able to:

  1. Explain a water balance (inflows, losses, abstractions, releases, change in storage) and use one to reason about a shared resource.
  2. Identify the competing demands on a multipurpose water body and the trade-offs between supply reliability, flood safety, livelihoods and ecosystems.
  3. Describe how allocation rules (proportional sharing, priority to drinking water, priority to irrigation) shift the burden of shortage between users.
  4. Recognise externalities: how one stakeholder's decision imposes costs on another (for example, flood releases onto downstream communities).
  5. Assess the value and limits of seasonal forecasts and monitoring data in decision-making under uncertainty.
  6. Reflect on the institutions IWRM calls for: stakeholder participation, transparent rules, compensation and basin-level coordination.
  7. Explain how nonpoint-source pollution from farms and mining moves through a water system, and why the same pollution load is more harmful in a drought.
  8. Recognise that a basin can be harmed by an upstream basin it cannot regulate, and argue for interagency and interbasin cooperation.

03Principles and theory

3.1 Integrated water resources management

The Global Water Partnership defines IWRM as a process that promotes the coordinated development and management of water, land and related resources to maximise economic and social welfare equitably, without compromising the sustainability of vital ecosystems. The game puts each part of that definition into a role:

IWRM ideaWhere it appears in the game
Water is finite and vulnerable (Dublin Principle 1)A single storage that every user draws from; droughts drain it and floods threaten it.
Participatory approach involving users, planners and policy-makers (Principle 2)Six teams with their own goals negotiate before each season and vote on the allocation rule.
Water has an economic value in all its competing uses (Principle 4)Rice and sugar profits depend on water delivered; GWI scores on supply reliability; Mahaica loses money to flooding.
EquityThe allocation rule decides who absorbs shortages; releases decide who absorbs floods.
Environmental sustainabilityThe Environment team protects a minimum storage, and the wetland is scored on its health.
Coordination across sectorsSupply, irrigation, flood safety and ecology are managed from one storage, so no team can optimise alone.

3.2 Common-pool resources

The conservancy is a common-pool resource: it is hard to exclude users, and water one team takes is water another cannot use. Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" predicts over-use when each user acts alone. Elinor Ostrom's work showed that communities often avoid that outcome through clear rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions and forums for resolving conflict. In the game, over-planting rice when the forecast looks good is the tragedy; the council's allocation rule and side agreements are the institutional response.

3.3 Upstream–downstream externalities

When the operator releases water to protect the dam, the benefit (dam safety for the coast and Georgetown) goes to many people and the cost (flooded farms) falls on a few downstream. This is a classic negative externality. The game makes it visible and lets teams experiment with responses: release limits, timing releases ahead of forecast rain, or compensation paid to Mahaica.

3.4 Storage, risk and the dual hazard

A conservancy must be kept low enough to absorb heavy rain and high enough to get through a dry season. These goals pull in opposite directions. The operator works inside a band between the wetland-health level and the safe operating limit. Holding water "just in case" raises flood risk; releasing early raises drought risk. That is the core tension of reservoir operation.

3.5 Decisions under uncertainty

Teams see a seasonal rainfall outlook (El Niño, Normal or La Niña) before deciding. By default it is right 70% of the time. Players must decide how much to trust it, which shows the value of good hydrometeorological monitoring and forecasting, and the cost of acting on a wrong forecast.

3.6 Serious games and participatory modelling

The game belongs to a family of role-play simulations used in water management, including the River Basin Game, Irrigania and the Wat-A-Game toolkit. These rest on experiential learning theory: people learn by acting, observing the result and reflecting on it. In companion modelling, stakeholders and a simple model learn together, and the model's rules are kept transparent so players can question them. For that reason every equation in the game is published here.

3.7 Nonpoint-source pollution and eutrophication

Fertilizer that crops do not take up is washed off fields by rain and drainage. Unlike a pipe from a factory, it comes from thousands of hectares at once, which makes it a nonpoint source: hard to measure, hard to regulate, and largest in wet seasons. Excess nitrogen and phosphorus in water feed algae. Algal blooms block light, and when the algae die and decay they use up dissolved oxygen, which can kill fish (eutrophication). Some blooms produce toxins that harm livestock drinking the water. The game tracks nitrogen as a single indicator of this process.

3.8 Sediment and mining

Gold mining and land clearing loosen soil that rivers carry as suspended sediment. Muddy water smothers fish habitat and riverbeds, cuts the light aquatic plants need, and costs water utilities more to treat. In Guyana, mining also raises concerns about mercury, which the game leaves out to stay simple but which belongs in the debrief.

3.9 Dilution: why drought and pollution go together

A concentration is a mass divided by a volume. If the same pollution load enters a conservancy that holds half as much water, the concentration doubles. Droughts therefore make water quality worse even if nobody pollutes more, and decisions that draw the conservancy down (heavy irrigation, large releases) also concentrate what is left. Quantity and quality cannot be managed separately.

3.10 Upstream basins and interbasin governance

Water does not respect administrative boundaries. A catchment's farms and mines may sit in a different region, under a different agency (agriculture, mining, forestry), or across a national border, while the costs land downstream. Downstream managers can do excellent work and still be harmed by decisions they have no authority over. The game's upstream switch makes this concrete. The IWRM response is coordination across agencies and basins: shared monitoring, joint planning, agreed standards, and sometimes payments for ecosystem services, in which downstream beneficiaries pay upstream land users to protect water.

06Rules of play

6.1 Setup

  • Split participants into six teams (2–5 people each). Hand each team its role card from section 5.
  • Project the game. One facilitator enters every decision on the shared screen.
  • Check that Settings show the climate script and parameters you want. Restarting with the same seed reproduces the same weather.

6.2 Each season

  1. Outlook. The facilitator reads out the season, its typical inflow and evaporation, and the rainfall outlook.
  2. Negotiation (5–8 minutes). Teams talk freely. They may make promises, threats, deals and side payments, but promises are not enforced by the model.
  3. Council vote. The council picks the allocation rule for this season by majority (or another procedure the group agrees).
  4. Decisions. Each team gives its decision to the facilitator, who sets the sliders. Decisions are final once the season runs.
  5. Run. The facilitator clicks Run this season. The actual climate is revealed.
  6. Report (3–5 minutes). Read out the water balance, the outcomes table and the event log. Each team states what it gained or lost.

6.3 Other rules

  • Decisions carry over to the next season unless a team changes them. Leak repair must be chosen again each season it is wanted.
  • Rice farmers may change their area and fertilizer only in a sowing window: the short wet season (spring crop) and the long wet season (autumn crop), and in season 1 to set the crop already in the ground. In the other seasons the game locks their controls, because the crop is already planted.
  • Undo last season exists to fix data-entry mistakes, not to replay bad luck.
  • Optional compensation rule: teams may agree to transfer points (for example, GWI pays Mahaica for accepting a release). The facilitator records transfers by hand and adjusts final scores.
  • The game ends after the last season (default 8). The basin total and each team's score feed into the debrief. There is no single winner; ask whether the basin as a whole did well.

6.4 Running the upstream basin

The facilitator sets the upstream basin each season with the Upstream is selector:

SettingFarmlandFertilizerMiningUse it to

Suggested sequence: seasons 1–4 on current practice; switch to expansion for seasons 5–6 without telling the downstream teams why their water is getting worse; then seat two upstream teams for seasons 7–8 and allow negotiation across the basin boundary, including side payments. Alternatively, run the whole game twice with the same seed, once downstream-only and once with upstream teams, and compare the charts.

Upstream teams are scored on their own earnings only. They feel none of the downstream harm, which is the point: any change in their behaviour has to come from negotiation, payment or regulation by a body with authority over both basins.

07Model and equations

The model is a lumped, single-reservoir water balance with a seasonal time step. All quantities are in Mm³ per season. The calculations run in this order each season t.

7.1 Inflow

Qt = Q̄s × cQ(kt) × (1 + εt)
(1)

where Q̄s is the base inflow for season type s (long dry, long wet, short dry, short wet), kt is the actual climate state, cQ is 0.4 for El Niño, 1.0 for Normal and 1.7 for La Niña, and εt is random noise drawn uniformly from [−0.15, +0.15].

7.2 Evaporation

Et = Ēs × cE(kt)
(2)

where Ēs is the base evaporation for the season and cE is 1.2 in El Niño seasons and 1.0 otherwise. Evaporation does not depend on storage, a simplification noted in section 12.

7.3 Protected floor and available water

Ft = max(Sdead, Senv,t)
(3)
At = max(0, St + Qt − Et − Ft)
(4)

where St is storage at the start of the season, Senv,t is the Environment team's protected minimum storage, and At is the water that can be given to users without going below the floor.

7.4 Demands

dGWI = requestGWI    drice = arice × wrice × φs    dsugar = asugar × wsugar × φs
(5)

where a is area in thousand hectares, w is the water requirement per thousand hectares in a fully dry season (rice 4.0, sugar 2.5 Mm³), and φs is the crop water factor for the season. For sugar it is 1.0 in dry seasons, 0.5 in the long wet season and 0.6 in the short wet season, because rain supplies part of the crop's needs. For rice it follows the crop calendar in section 9.1: 0.7 at spring-crop sowing (short wet), 1.0 while the spring crop matures in the long dry season, 0.6 at autumn-crop sowing (long wet) and 0.5 in the short dry season, when the autumn crop is drained for harvest and fields lie fallow in November.

GWI's real need is separate from its request:

nGWI,t = max(0, nGWI − Δn × Lt)
(6)

where nGWI = 15 Mm³ is the base need, Δn = 2 Mm³ is the saving per leak repair, and Lt is the number of repairs made in earlier seasons. A repair takes effect from the following season.

7.5 Allocation

The operator first sets how much of each user's demand to supply, as a share σi between 0 and 100%:

qi = di × σi
(6b)

Water the operator does not allocate (di − qi) stays in the conservancy. If the total allocation Σq ≤ At, every user receives its allocation in full. Otherwise the council's rule shares out At, applied to the allocations qi in place of the demands di below:

Proportional:   Di = di × At / Σd
(7a)
Drinking water first:   DGWI = min(dGWI, At); the remainder At − DGWI is shared proportionally between rice and sugar
(7b)
Irrigation first:   rice and sugar share At proportionally up to their demand; GWI receives what remains
(7c)

Total delivered water is Dt = DGWI + Drice + Dsugar, and the deficit is Σd − Dt, measured against what users needed, not what they were allocated. Users are scored on the share of their need that was met, so an operator's cut costs them yield or reliability whether it came from scarcity or from a decision to save water.

7.6 Release

Deliveries come first; the operator's release is then limited by the sluice capacity and by the water left above dead storage:

Rt = min( rt, Rcap, max(0, St + Qt − Et − Dt − Sdead) )
(8)

where rt is the operator's requested release and Rcap = 150 Mm³ is the sluice capacity. The release may take storage below the Environment floor: the floor binds deliveries only, which is a deliberate source of tension.

7.7 Storage update and overtopping

S′ = St + Qt − Et − Dt − Rt
(9)
Ot = max(0, S′ − Smax)     St+1 = S′ − Ot
(10)

Any overtopping Ot > 0 is a breach: uncontrolled water floods the coast. Combining (9) and (10), the full balance for each season is:

ΔS = St+1 − St = Qt − Et − Dt − Rt − Ot
(11)

This is the "Water balance" table shown after each season. It always closes: start + inflow − evaporation − delivered − released − overtopped = end.

7.8 Upstream land use and inflow

Clearing land for farms and mines makes runoff faster: more water arrives in wet seasons and less in dry seasons. Equation (1) is multiplied by:

mt = 1 + 0.01 aup + 0.03 g  (wet seasons)     mt = max(0.5, 1 − 0.008 aup − 0.015 g)  (dry seasons)
(12)

where aup is upstream farmland in thousand ha and g is the mining scale (0 none, 1 small, 2 medium, 3 large).

7.9 Pollution loads into the conservancy

Loads are in tonnes per season. Because 1 tonne per Mm³ equals 1 mg/L, masses and volumes combine directly into concentrations.

LN = cN,bg Qt + aup × Nup(rate) × xs
(13)

where cN,bg = 0.4 mg/L is the natural nitrogen in catchment runoff, Nup is fertilizer applied (60, 120 or 200 kg N/ha; thousand ha × kg/ha = tonnes), and xs is the share washed off: 0.25 in wet seasons, 0.08 in dry seasons.

LTSS = cT,bg Qt + 300 aup ws + G(g) × hs × (1 − 0.7 [ponds])
(14)

where cT,bg = 8 mg/L is natural sediment, 300 t per thousand ha is erosion from cleared farmland (ws = 1 wet, 0.3 dry), G is mining sediment (0, 5,000, 14,000 or 30,000 t by scale), and hs = 1.5 in wet seasons and 0.5 in dry seasons. Settling ponds remove 70%.

7.10 Mixing, removal and concentration

The conservancy is treated as well mixed. The pollutant mass already in it and the new load are combined, a fixed share is removed (nitrogen by plant uptake and denitrification, sediment by settling), and the remainder is spread through the water present before withdrawals:

CN,t = (MN,t + LN)(1 − kN) / (St + Qt − Et)     CT,t = (MT,t + LTSS)(1 − kT) / (St + Qt − Et)
(15)

where M is the pollutant mass in the conservancy at the start of the season, kN = 0.3 and kT = 0.6. Evaporation removes water but not pollutant, so it concentrates what remains.

Every outflow (deliveries, releases, overtopping) leaves at this concentration, so the mass carried into the next season is:

Mt+1 = Ct × St+1
(16)

7.11 Nitrogen reaching the estuary

Coastal farm drainage bypasses the conservancy and goes to the sea. Released and overtopped conservancy water also reaches the coast through the Mahaica River:

Ncoast = (arice Nrice + asugar Nsugar) × xs × (1 − 0.3 [buffer]) + (Rt + Ot) × CN,t
(17)

where Nrice is the rice fertilizer applied in the season: 70, 120 or 170 kg N/ha per crop by rate, of which 60% is applied in the sowing season and 40% in the following season. Nsugar is 30, 60 or 90 kg N/ha per season. Buffer strips cut farm runoff by 30%. Under the EPA fertilizer cap, a "high" choice is applied as "medium".

where α is Mahaica's floodplain activity as a fraction (0–1). Breach penalty: in any season with overtopping, every team except the operator loses a further 50 points. The operator's −100 already reflects it.

Why the scores are built this way

  • Rice and sugar pay their costs on every hectare planted but earn revenue only on the share that is watered. Over-planting in a drought therefore loses money, which rewards realistic planting.
  • GWI is scored against its need, not its request. Asking for more than it needs takes water from others without earning GWI anything.
  • Mahaica's damage grows with both the excess release and how much it has put on the floodplain, so the community shares responsibility for its exposure.
  • The operator is rewarded for safety and for supply together, so neither "drain it" nor "fill it" is a winning strategy.
  • Fertilizer is privately profitable: high fertilizer earns rice farmers about 0.7 more points per thousand ha than medium when water is plentiful. The cost lands on the estuary, through the Environment team's score. That is an externality the players have to resolve.
  • Upstream teams feel no downstream harm, so the game rewards them for polluting unless the downstream teams negotiate or pay.
  • The basin totals (downstream and upstream, shown separately) are rough measures of collective welfare. Debate whether they are fair.

9.1 Rice crop calendar

Guyana grows two rice crops a year. Following the Guyana Rice Development Board (GRDB), the first (spring) crop is sown in November–December and harvested in March–April, and the second (autumn) crop is sown around May–June and reaped in September–October. The GRDB advises sowing between 15 November and 31 December for the first crop and between 15 May and 30 June for the second, so that the crop flowers when sunlight is strongest. Sowing is timed to the start of the rainy seasons, while harvesting falls in the dry seasons. Rain helps flood and puddle the fields, but two crops a year still depend on reliable irrigation, because rain is irregular and dry spells are common.

In the game, each crop spans two seasons. Rice farmers choose area and fertilizer in the sowing season and are locked into them for the following season. They must commit before they know whether the outlook is right: an El Niño arriving after sowing leaves a large crop to be carried through a dry season. In the real calendar, sowing starts in mid-November at the end of the short dry season. The game counts it in the short wet season for simplicity.

The default climate script for the eight seasons is: Normal, La Niña, Normal, Normal, El Niño, Normal, El Niño, La Niña. The early La Niña long-wet season tests flood management; the later El Niño dry seasons test drought management. In random mode each season is El Niño with probability 0.25, Normal 0.5 and La Niña 0.25.

Forecast: with probability p (default 0.7) the outlook equals the actual climate; otherwise it shows one of the other two states at random. Weather is generated from a fixed random seed, so two groups playing with the same seed face identical weather and forecasts, which makes groups comparable.

11Worked examples

All examples use the default settings, with the upstream basin on current practice unless stated, and the starting decisions: GWI requests 15, rice 15k ha and sugar 8k ha on medium fertilizer, Environment reserve 90 with no farm rule, Mahaica activity 60%. Every number was produced by the game's own model.

11.1 Season 1: a normal long dry season

Start storage 200. The seeded noise is −5.7%, and current upstream land use trims dry-season inflow by 4.7% (equation 12), so inflow is 40 × 1.0 × 0.943 × 0.953 = 35.9; evaporation is 35. The floor is max(60, 90) = 90, so available water is 200 + 35.9 − 35 − 90 = 110.9.

Demands: GWI 15; rice 15 × 4.0 × 1.0 = 60, because the spring crop is maturing; sugar 8 × 2.5 × 1.0 = 20; total 95. That is less than 110.9, so every user is fully supplied. With a release of 20, end storage is 200 + 35.9 − 35 − 95 − 20 = 85.9.

Scores: GWI 30; rice 15 × (10 − 4) = 90; sugar 8 × (8 − 3) = 40; operator 20 + 10 = 30; Mahaica 40 × 0.6 = 24; Environment −5 (−25 because 85.9 is below the wetland level of 120, +10 for clean conservancy water, +10 for a healthy estuary). Lesson: full supply in a dry season can leave the ecosystem, and the next season, short.

11.3 Season 5: El Niño drought and the allocation rule

Suppose storage is 150 at the start of the El Niño long dry season, with the spring rice crop at its thirstiest (release 0). Inflow is 40 × 0.4 × 0.863 × 0.953 = 13.2; evaporation is 35 × 1.2 = 42. Available above the floor of 90: 150 + 13.2 − 42 − 90 = 31.2 against demand of 95.

Lowering the Environment reserve would release more water but stress the wetland.

12Assumptions and limits

This is a teaching model. It is deliberately simple so players can follow it. Do not use its numbers for design, operation or planning decisions.

  • Lumped storage: one well-mixed reservoir; no spatial detail, canals, conveyance losses or travel times.
  • Seasonal time step: peaks inside a season (a three-day storm) are averaged out. Real overtopping risk depends on daily or hourly inflows.
  • No level–area–volume curve: storage is used directly; evaporation does not shrink as the water surface shrinks.
  • Inflow is exogenous: runoff is a seasonal base value times a climate factor, not calculated from rainfall, soils and land use.
  • No groundwater, no water quality, no return flows from irrigation, and no drainage from the coastal lands into the conservancy.
  • Fixed order of operations: deliveries are made before releases each season.
  • Simple economics: linear revenue and cost per hectare, flat damage per Mm³; points are not dollars.
  • Forecast skill is uniform across seasons, and climate states are independent from one season to the next.
  • Two quality indicators only: nitrogen stands in for all nutrients (phosphorus is often the limiting nutrient in fresh water) and suspended sediment for mining impacts. Mercury, pesticides, bacteria, dissolved oxygen and salinity are not modelled.
  • Fully mixed conservancy: real conservancies have stagnant corners and plumes near inflows; concentrations near intakes can differ greatly from the average.
  • Constant removal rates: uptake and settling do not vary with temperature, residence time or depth, and settled sediment never resuspends.
  • No load from the coastal lands into the conservancy, and coastal farm runoff goes straight to the estuary with no delay or treatment in drainage canals.

13Using real data and WEAP

The game is not built on WEAP, but the two work well together.

13.1 Calibrating the game

  1. Gather conservancy storage at key levels (dead, safe operating, crest) from the level–volume relationship, and convert to Mm³.
  2. Estimate seasonal inflow from gauged or modelled runoff, and evaporation from pan or Penman–Monteith estimates multiplied by surface area.
  3. Set demands from abstraction records: GWI treatment-plant intake, irrigated areas and seasonal crop water requirements.
  4. Set climate factors from historical El Niño and La Niña seasons compared with normal seasons, using hydrometeorological records.
  5. Enter the values under Settings and test a full game to check the balance behaves sensibly.

Water quality: use raw-water monitoring at GWI intakes and EPA or GGMC sampling for nitrogen and turbidity or TSS; fertilizer application rates from extension services or farmer surveys; mining footprint from satellite imagery (the land-cover change methods used in GIS courses apply directly). Calibrate the removal rates kN and kT so the model reproduces observed seasonal concentrations.

13.2 Linking to WEAP

  • WEAP → game: build a WEAP model of the system with the conservancy as a reservoir and GWI, rice and sugar as demand sites. Run it for typical, wet and dry years and use the seasonal totals as the game's inflows and demands.
  • Game → WEAP: export the game's results as CSV. Take the planting areas, reserve level and allocation rule the teams settled on, and test them as a WEAP scenario with full hydrology and a monthly or daily time step. WEAP's demand priorities correspond to the game's allocation rules.
  • Water quality in WEAP: WEAP can carry pollutant loads with demand sites and catchments and has a link to QUAL2K, so the game's quality scenarios can also be tested there.
  • Workshop sequence: play the game first to build intuition about trade-offs, then train participants to build the same system in WEAP, then compare the two.

14Facilitation and debrief

14.1 Suggested timetable (about 2 hours)

TimeActivity
0:00–0:15Introduce IWRM, the conservancy and the roles. Teams read their role cards.
0:15–0:25Practice season with the facilitator explaining each screen. Then restart.
0:25–1:30Eight seasons at about 8 minutes each.
1:30–2:00Debrief, using the storage chart and the event log.

14.2 Debrief questions

  • Experience: How did your team feel during the flood season and the drought? Which decision do you regret?
  • System: Looking at the storage chart, when did the basin get into trouble, and which earlier decisions set that up?
  • Equity: Who carried the cost of protecting the dam? Was it agreed or imposed? Should Mahaica be compensated, and by whom?
  • Rules: Which allocation rule did you choose, and did it change in the drought? Which is fairest? How does it compare with Guyana's law and practice?
  • Information: How much did you trust the forecast? What monitoring, forecasting or data sharing would have helped?
  • Institutions: What body should make these decisions in reality? What would a basin council for the conservancy need to work?
  • Quality: When did water quality get worse, and was it because of more pollution or less water? How could you tell the difference?
  • Upstream: How did it feel to lose points to a basin you could not influence? What changed once the upstream teams were at the table? Which agencies in Guyana would need to sit on a body that covers both basins?
  • Operator: When did the operator cut someone's supply? Was it to save water for a forecast drought, and did that drought arrive? Who should be able to overrule the operator?
  • Payments: Did anyone pay the upstream miners to use settling ponds, or the coastal farms to use less fertilizer? Who should pay: the polluter or the beneficiary?
  • Transfer: Where do you see the same pattern in your own work?

14.3 Variations

  • No talking: play two seasons without negotiation, then allow it, and compare.
  • Perfect forecast: set accuracy to 1.0 to show the value of information; set it to 0.33 for no skill.
  • Climate change: lower dry-season inflows or raise La Niña frequency in the script.
  • Growing city: raise GWI's base need mid-game to model population growth.
  • Upstream ambush: switch the upstream basin to expansion halfway through without announcing it, and see how long downstream teams take to find the cause.
  • Two runs, one seed: play once downstream-only and once with upstream teams, and compare the quality charts.
  • Assessment: ask students to write a short policy brief recommending an operating rule, supported by their game data.

14.4 Playing with several tables

A team is one stakeholder role, played by one or more people. A table is one complete basin: a full set of roles running its own copy of the game. There are two ways to organise the room.

One basin, one table per roleSeveral basins, each table plays every role
Group size6–24 people (1–4 per role), plus 2 upstream tables if usedAny size, in tables of 6–10 (1–2 people per role)
ScreensOne projector; the facilitator enters every decisionOne laptop or tablet per table, all opening the same GitHub link
StrengthLoud, visible negotiation across the roomSeveral outcomes to compare under identical weather
WeaknessOne outcome only; large groups sit idleLess cross-room drama

A useful hybrid for 20–30 participants: play seasons 1–4 as one basin on the projector so everyone learns the game, then split into 3–4 tables for seasons 5–8 and compare.

Running several tables at once. The game runs entirely in each browser, so any number of tables can open the same link at the same time and each gets an independent game. Internet is only needed to load the page. Give each table these rules:

  • Enter a table name at the top before starting.
  • Do not change Settings. All tables share the same seed, so they face the same weather, forecasts and forecast errors.
  • Do not refresh or close the tab: the game does not save, and reloading starts over.
  • Use Undo only to correct a typing mistake.
  • Each person argues for their own role, not for the table's total.
  • Switch the upstream basin only when the facilitator announces it to the whole room.

Collecting and comparing results. When a table finishes, it uses Download results file and sends the file to the facilitator by WhatsApp, email or USB, or uses Copy results code and pastes the code into a message. The facilitator opens the Compare tables page on the projector and drops in the files or pastes the codes. Nothing is uploaded anywhere: the comparison happens on the facilitator's computer, and it works offline from a local copy.

The comparison page shows a scorecard ranked by any column (share of demand met, dam breaches, Mahaica flood seasons, seasons of poor conservancy and coastal water, downstream and upstream points), the storage and quality curves of every table on shared charts, points by role, and the main choices each table made. It warns if tables used different settings, played different numbers of seasons, or set the upstream basin differently.

The basin health index combines the scorecard into one number: 100 × share of demand met − 25 per dam breach − 10 per season of Mahaica flooding, poor conservancy water or poor coastal water. Treat it as a prompt for discussion rather than a verdict: the table with the most points is often not the healthiest basin, and that contrast makes a strong debrief.

15Glossary

ConservancyA large shallow reservoir behind an earthen dam on Guyana's coast, storing water for supply and irrigation and protecting the coast from inland flooding.
Mm³Million cubic metres. 1 Mm³ is 1,000,000 m³, or 1 billion litres.
Water balanceAccounting of all water entering and leaving a system; the difference is the change in storage.
Dead storageWater below the lowest outlet, which cannot be withdrawn.
Relief sluiceA gated structure used to release water from the conservancy to a river to lower its level.
OvertoppingWater flowing over the crest of a dam; it erodes earthen dams and can cause failure.
ExternalityA cost or benefit that falls on someone who did not make the decision.
ENSOEl Niño–Southern Oscillation. In coastal Guyana El Niño tends to bring drier conditions and La Niña wetter ones.
Nonpoint-source pollutionPollution that comes from a wide area, such as fertilizer runoff from fields, rather than from a single pipe.
EutrophicationOver-enrichment of water with nutrients, leading to algal blooms, oxygen depletion and fish kills.
TSSTotal suspended solids: the sediment carried in water, measured in mg/L. High TSS makes water muddy.
KokerA sluice gate in Guyana's sea defences and drainage canals that lets water drain to the sea at low tide.
Buffer stripA strip of vegetation left between fields and a drain or waterway to trap sediment and nutrients.
Payment for ecosystem servicesAn arrangement in which those who benefit from clean water pay land users upstream to protect it.
Common-pool resourceA resource from which it is hard to exclude users and whose use by one reduces what is left for others.
