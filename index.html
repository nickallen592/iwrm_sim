Conservancy Council

An IWRM role-play for six downstream stakeholder teams, plus two optional upstream teams, sharing one coastal water conservancy. Each season the teams negotiate, set their decisions, and the model returns the water balance, water quality, who went short, who flooded, and what reached the coast.

Event log

    Facilitator guide and model equations

    Running a session (about 90 minutes)

    1. Split participants into six teams and give each a role: GWI, Rice farmers, Sugar estate, Conservancy operator, Mahaica communities, Environment (EPA). Put this page on the projector; the facilitator enters decisions.
    2. Read out the season and the rainfall forecast. The forecast is right about 70% of the time, like a real seasonal outlook.
    3. Give teams 5–8 minutes to negotiate. The council (all teams together) agrees on the allocation rule. Each team then tells the facilitator its decision.
    4. Click Run this season. Read out the water balance and the events. Ask who gained and who paid.
    5. Play 8 seasons (two years). The default climate script has a La Niña flood season early and El Niño droughts later, so teams face both too much and too little water.

    Water quality and the upstream basin

    Coastal rice and sugar choose a fertilizer rate. Their runoff drains to the estuary through canals and kokers, not into the conservancy. The conservancy's own quality depends on the upstream catchment: farm fertilizer adds nitrogen, mining and cleared land add sediment. Polluted water raises GWI's treatment costs and harms Mahaica livestock when it is released, and nitrogen reaching the estuary threatens marine life.

    Suggested run: play seasons 1–4 with upstream fixed at current practice, switch to expansion for seasons 5–6 without warning, then hand the upstream basin to two teams for seasons 7–8 and let everyone negotiate across the basin boundary.

    Debrief questions

    • Who carried the cost when the operator released water to protect the dam? Was that agreed or imposed?
    • Did any team over-plant or over-request because the forecast looked good? What happened when the forecast was wrong?
    • How did the allocation rule change who went short? Which rule is fairest, and which is in Guyana's law and practice?
    • What data or institutions (gauges, forecasts, a basin council, compensation for Mahaica) would have changed your decisions?
    • Which team's score best reflects the public interest? Is the basin total a fair measure?
    • What changed when the upstream basin got a seat at the table? Which agency or forum should host that conversation in reality?
    • Coastal farmers earned more with high fertilizer while the estuary paid. Who should bear that cost, and what would make them change?

    Model equations (per season, in million m³)

    Inflow = base inflow × climate factor × (1 ± noise); climate factor is 0.4 for El Niño, 1.0 for Normal, 1.7 for La Niña. El Niño also raises evaporation by 20%.

    Floor = max(dead storage, Environment reserve). Available = Start + Inflow − Evaporation − Floor.

    Demands: GWI = its request; Rice = area (thousand ha) × rice rate × season crop factor; Sugar likewise. If demands exceed Available, the council rule shares the shortfall.

    Release = min(operator request, sluice capacity, storage above dead storage). End = Start + Inflow − Evaporation − Delivered − Release. Anything above the dam crest overtops and counts as a breach.

    Points: GWI 30 × share of need met (leak repair costs 15 and cuts need by 2 for the rest of the game). Rice and Sugar earn area × (revenue × share met − cost). Operator +20 in the safe band, −30 above the safe level, −100 for a breach, +10 if users got at least 90%. Mahaica earns income from floodplain activity minus damage from releases above the river's safe limit. Environment +25 if storage ends above the wetland-health level, otherwise −25. A breach costs every other team 50.
